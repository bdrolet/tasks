# Strategy-in-a-task-system: framework research

**Date:** 2026-10-09
**Status:** research notes, input to a future design spec. Not committed as a decision.

Scope note: this is a literature scan, not a design. Sources are mostly practitioner material; where the evidence is thin or the framework is marketing, it says so. Nothing below applies cleanly to one person out of the box — every framework assumes a team, so "solo fit" is a judgment.

---

## 1. Goal/strategy frameworks

**Synthesis.** The frameworks split into three structural families: *metric trees* (OKRs, strategy maps, 4DX), *necessity trees* (TOC goal tree, theory of change, impact mapping, OST), and *narrative/diagnostic* (Rumelt, Working Backwards, Wardley, GTD horizons). Only the necessity-tree family has a native notion of "this node is required for the goal" — and only Dettmer's Goal Tree makes that an explicit test per node. Everything else leaves "is this task on the path" to judgment.

- **OKRs** — Objective (qualitative) + 3–5 measurable Key Results; tasks are "initiatives" under KRs. Structure: shallow list, metric-anchored, quarterly. Good at: forcing a measurable definition of "done" and a 0.0–1.0 grade that calibrates ambition (Google norm ~0.6–0.7 on aspirational OKRs; a consistent 1.0 means the target was too easy — [whatmatters.com grading](https://www.whatmatters.com/okrs-explained/grading-okrs), [re:Work summary](https://modelmonkey.io/blog/google-rework-okrs-guide)). Bad at: solo use degenerates into task lists ("read 5 books") with self-graded, gameable targets — [Gothelf, individual vs personal OKRs](https://jeffgothelf.com/blog/individual-okrs-vs-personal-okrs/); Spotify dropped individual OKRs as overhead that didn't track direction changes — [Spotify HR blog](https://hrblog.spotify.com/2016/08/15/our-beliefs). No critical-path or necessary/optional notion; a KR is a target, not a dependency.
- **Rumelt's kernel** (diagnosis → guiding policy → coherent actions) and **The Crux** (find the one *addressable* hardest challenge). Structure: three paragraphs, not a tree. Its strongest contribution is negative space: the guiding policy says what you will *not* do, and "goals are not strategy" — a list of aspirations (land clients, fix taxes, handle family) is exactly what Rumelt calls bad strategy ([sachinrekhi summary](https://sachinrekhi.com/good-strategy-bad-strategy-richard-rumelt), [BCG interview on The Crux](https://bcghendersoninstitute.com/the-crux-with-richard-rumelt/)). Solo fit is excellent because it is cheap and forces one focal challenge; it has no task-level mechanics at all. "Coherent" is the closest thing to necessity: actions that don't reinforce the guiding policy are noise.
- **GTD Horizons of Focus** — six altitudes (purpose → vision → 1–2yr goals → areas → projects → next actions). Structure: layered lists, linked top-down only by the weekly review ([gettingthingsdone.com](https://gettingthingsdone.com/2018/12/the-gtd-horizons-of-focus-for-determining-your-priorities/)). Good at: the *next physical action* discipline and the rule that every project must have one. Bad at: there's no mechanism for a goal to *reject* a project — alignment is a human check, and Allen explicitly says to master the runway before the horizons. No critical path.
- **Opportunity Solution Tree** (Torres) — outcome → opportunities (needs/pains) → solutions → assumption tests ([producttalk glossary](https://www.producttalk.org/glossary-discovery-opportunity-solution-tree/)). Structure: a real tree with a measurable root. Strong on "which branch am I betting on" and on forcing ≥3 solutions per opportunity so you don't frame a solution as the problem. Built for product discovery with customers; for "land a client" the opportunities are client pains, which actually maps well. No critical path, but the tree makes *unbacked* branches visible.
- **Impact Mapping** (Adzic) — why (goal) → who (actors) → how (behaviour change) → what (deliverables) ([draft.io explainer](https://site.draft.io/impact-mapping/), [EBG review](https://ebgconsulting.com/?p=941)). Structure: mind map. Its test — "which actor's behaviour does this deliverable change, toward which goal?" — is a usable necessity filter for tasks: a task with no actor/impact path is busywork. Light enough for one person.
- **TOC Goal Tree / thinking processes** (Dettmer) — Goal → 3–5 Critical Success Factors → Necessary Conditions, edges read "is necessary to achieve", and each CSF must pass "can I reach the goal without this? if yes, cut it" ([Flying Logic guide](https://flyinglogic.com/1442/how-to-create-a-goal-tree-with-flying-logic/), [TP overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thinking_processes_(theory_of_constraints))). Current Reality Tree finds the root cause behind undesirable effects; Prerequisite Tree orders obstacles/intermediate objectives by dependency. **This is the only framework in the set with an explicit necessary-vs-optional test and a dependency-ordered plan.** Cost: heavy diagramming; Dettmer himself caps it at 2–3 NC levels. Evidence is practitioner-only.
- **Strategy maps** (Kaplan/Norton) — four perspectives linked by cause-effect arrows ([thinkinsights](https://thinkinsights.net/strategy/strategy-maps-drive-execution)). The causal arrows are the weakness: Nørreklit's 2000 critique argues they're assumed, not demonstrated. Corporate; skip for solo.
- **Theory of change** — backwards-map from long-term outcome to preconditions, with assumptions tracked separately as "necessary but already true" ([theoryofchange.org](https://www.theoryofchange.org/?p=235), [DIME wiki](https://dimewiki.worldbank.org/Theory_of_Change)). Structurally the same as a goal tree but with the explicit test "plausible, doable, testable." The precondition/assumption split is directly reusable.
- **Working Backwards (PR/FAQ)** — write the launch press release first ([koji guide](https://www.koji.so/docs/working-backwards-pr-faq-guide), [ModelThinkers critique](https://www.modelthinkers.com/mental-model/amazons-working-backwards)). Good for defining what "landed the consulting practice" looks like in a paragraph; critiques say it front-loads work and needs reviewers the solo operator doesn't have. No task mechanics.
- **Wardley maps** — value-chain vs evolution axis; situational awareness. Critiques: y-axis is a convention not a measure; socially constructed ([beza1e1](https://beza1e1.tuxen.de/wardley_maps.html), [multidisciplined](https://multidisciplined.substack.com/p/wardley-maps-and-me-and-you-should)). Useful once to decide *what to build vs buy* in a consulting offer; useless day-to-day.

## 2. Critical path for knowledge work

**Synthesis.** CPM/PERT/Critical Chain all require a fixed activity graph with duration estimates; the literature's own critiques concede the estimates are unreliable, but the deeper problem for goal-directed work is that *the graph itself is unknown* — "land a client" has no decomposition until you pick a theory of how clients get landed. The usable substitutes are (a) necessity trees that give you a *partial order* without durations, (b) GTD's "next action" that commits only to one edge, and (c) treating the current "theory of the path" as a hypothesis.

- **CPM/PERT assumptions**: well-defined tasks, independent durations, normal totals, no path interaction; these produce "false precision" on novel work ([pmknowledgecenter critical note](http://www.pmknowledgecenter.com/dynamic_scheduling/baseline/critical-note-pert-manual-simulation-study), [Atlassian CPM](https://www.atlassian.com/work-management/project-management/critical-path-method)).
- **Critical Chain** (Goldratt): strip padding from tasks, pool it into a project buffer, and use *buffer burn rate* as the control signal instead of milestone hits; student syndrome and Parkinson's Law explain why padded estimates still slip ([eight2late intro](https://eight2late.com/2009/08/20/an-introduction-to-the-critical-chain-method), [projectmanagement.com](https://www.projectmanagement.com/articles/234283/chain-reaction)). Transferable idea: *one* buffer per goal and watch its consumption, rather than per-task due dates. Still assumes a graph.
- **No literature found** on "critical path" for outcome-based goals as such. The closest is TOC's Prerequisite Tree (obstacle → intermediate objective, dependency-ordered) and theory-of-change pathways. The honest framing: the critical path for "land a client" is a *hypothesis* (e.g., niche → offer → 10 named prospects → conversations → proposal), and the path gets re-derived as evidence arrives. The consulting-coaching sources agree on that rough sequence but it's heuristic, not evidence ([MBO Partners 10 steps](https://www.mbopartners.com/?p=33130), [solopreneur stack](https://thesolopreneurstack.substack.com/p/attracting-your-first-clients-creating)).
- **Next physical action** (GTD): "you can't complete a project, only the next step" — a project with no next action is stalled by definition ([facilethings weekly review](https://facilethings.com/blog/en/basics-weekly-review)). This is the minimal, robust form of critical path: one edge at a time.
- **The One Thing** (Keller): "what's the one thing that makes everything else easier or unnecessary?" — rhetorically useful, evidence anecdotal ([SuperSummary](https://www.supersummary.com/the-one-thing/summary/)). The "unnecessary" clause is the useful bit: it's a test for which task collapses others.
- **80/20** (Koch): heuristic, not a law; Koch admits causes "can't be isolated" ([Shortform overview](https://www.shortform.com/blog/the-80-20-principle-richard-koch/)). No controlled study of the time-allocation claim found.
- **"Minimum viable plan"**: the phrase has no established source; nearest is MVP/lean advice ("overbuilding is the common solo mistake") ([bootstrapped founder](https://thebootstrappedfounder.com/avoiding-the-entrepreneurial-planning-trap/)).

## 3. De-risking / assumption-driven planning

**Synthesis.** Every method here does the same thing: enumerate what must be true, rank by (importance × lack of evidence), and spend the next unit of effort on the top one. That ranking *is* the answer to "what can I do now to de-risk goal X" — the de-risking task is "run the cheapest test of the riskiest untested assumption."

- **Assumption mapping / RAT** (Bland, *Testing Business Ideas*): plot desirability/viability/feasibility assumptions on importance × evidence; test the top-right (important, unevidenced) first ([Strategyzer](https://strategyzer.com/library/how-assumptions-mapping-can-focus-your-teams-on-running-experiments-that-matter), [Mural intro](https://mural.co/blog/intro-assumptions-mapping), critique of Bland vs Gothelf variants at [productcompass](https://www.productcompass.pm/p/assumption-prioritization-canvas)). Directly task-generating: each top assumption → one test task with a pass/fail threshold.
- **Discovery-Driven Planning** (McGrath/MacMillan 1995): reverse income statement (required profit → required revenue → required client count → required conversion), explicit assumption checklist, funding released at milestones that *test* assumptions ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discovery-driven_planning), [Burleson summary](https://scottburleson.substack.com/p/article-summary-discovery-driven)). For a consultancy: "need $X/mo → N clients at $Y → M proposals → K conversations → J outreach/week" turns the goal into a checkable chain of numbers with the weakest-evidence link first.
- **Pre-mortem** (Klein): "it's a year later and this failed — why?" Based on Mitchell/Russo/Pennington 1989: prospective hindsight raised the *number and specificity* of reasons generated by ~30% — not forecast accuracy, which secondary sources routinely overstate ([Uncertainty Project](https://theuncertaintyproject.org/tools/pre-mortem)). Cheap, solo-friendly, output is a list of failure modes that each imply a de-risking task.
- **Real options**: options have value, options expire, don't commit before the expiry unless you know why; the counter-literature says habitual option-hoarding is its own trap ([McDonald, agileconnection](https://agileconnection.com/article/using-real-options-decide-when-decide), [optionality trap](https://drhappy.com.au/?p=13547)). Practical form: tag each decision with its expiry date and let that drive urgency.
- **Shape Up** (Basecamp): fix appetite (time), vary scope; hunt "rabbit holes" before committing; vague "grab bag" scopes rejected ([danlebrero summary](https://danlebrero.com/2021/06/16/shape-up-summary/)). Appetite is a solo-usable idea: a goal gets a time budget, and overruns are signal, not failure.

## 4. Knowing when strategy needs to change

**Synthesis.** The common recommendation is to *pre-register* the condition that would change your mind, because in the moment you won't. The strongest versions pair a measurable state with a date. Cadenced reviews catch slow drift; tripwires catch discrete events; lead/lag divergence diagnoses *which* thing is wrong.

- **Tripwires** (Heath, *Decisive*): decide in advance "if X by date Y, we revisit"; counters autopilot and confirmation bias; forms: deadlines, budget caps, partitions ([Uncertainty Project tripwires](https://www.theuncertaintyproject.org/tools/tripwires)).
- **Kill criteria** (Duke, *Quit*): "states and dates" — "if I can't get an exec in the room by the next meeting, kill the deal"; Everest turnaround-time analogy. Critique: rigid preset windows can ignore learning ([Kill Criteria essay](https://elliotrobia.substack.com/p/kill-criteria), [sjhoward review](https://sjhoward.co.uk/?p=11672)).
- **Lead vs lag** (4DX): lead measures are controllable and predictive (outreach sent), lag is the result (clients signed). Diagnostic: lead strong + lag flat ⇒ *theory is wrong, change strategy*; lead weak ⇒ execution problem ([concepts.dsebastien lead/lag](https://concepts.dsebastien.net/concept/lead-and-lag-measures/)). This is the single cleanest "strategy needs to change" signal found.
- **Grove inflection points**: the trigger looks small; listen to Cassandras ([karthikchidambaram notes](https://www.karthikchidambaram.com/?p=9082)). Solo translation: your own pipeline anomalies are the Cassandra.
- **OODA** (Boyd): the loop is about *orientation* — destroying obsolete mental models and rebuilding ([macroops](https://macroops.substack.com/p/john-boyds-ooda-loop)). Mostly inspirational at the personal scale.
- **Belief updating**: superforecasters update in small increments; "strong opinions weakly held" fails because it doesn't say *what evidence* should move you — set explicit expectancies instead ([Commoncog](https://commoncog.com/strong-opinions-weakly-held-is-bad/), [Wharton on Tetlock](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/why-an-open-mind-is-key-to-making-better-predictions/)). Decision journals: record decision, reasoning, expected outcome, confidence *before* the outcome ([Eppo: decide before experimenting](https://geteppo.com/blog/make-decisions-before-experimenting)).
- **Cadences**: GTD weekly review (every project has a next action; projects checked against areas/goals); OKRs check in weekly but grade only at quarter end — whatmatters explicitly argues against weekly grading ([whatmatters FAQ](https://whatmatters.com/faqs/weekly-okr-grading-check-in)); CCPM buffer burn as continuous signal.

## 5. Deciding what NOT to do

**Synthesis.** Nobody has a formal model of "is this task necessary for any goal" except the TOC goal tree's per-node test and impact mapping's actor/impact chain. The rest are questions to ask: Drucker's, Keller's "unnecessary" clause, Collins's stop-doing list. Satisficing supplies the quality-bar half: "good enough" is a *set aspiration level*, revised with experience.

- **Drucker**: "What would happen if this were not done at all?" — if nothing, stop; plus "start with your time, not your tasks" ([Effective Executive notes](https://www.goodreads.com/notes/5539779-the-effective-executive/2680940-varun/391cfbc3-f05c-45e8-a992-499547d506e3)).
- **Stop-doing list** (Collins): great companies use it as much as the to-do list ([fs.blog](https://fs.blog/jim-collins-stop-doing-list/), [signalvnoise](https://signalvnoise.com/posts/2025-jim-collins-and-the-stop-doing-list)).
- **Satisficing** (Simon): choose the first option meeting an aspiration level; under *uncertainty* (not mere risk) it often beats optimization — Artinger/Gigerenzer/Jacobs 2022 JEL ([aeaweb](https://www.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/jel.20201396)). The accountant example is a satisficing call: the aspiration level is "confident enough to file," and the test is whether the check changes the action.
- **Eisenhower matrix limits**: subjective labels, ignores effort and dependencies, Q1 overload ([t2informatik](https://t2informatik.de/en/?p=20733), [projektron](https://www.projektron.de/en/blog/details/eisenhower-matrix-3687/)). WSJF already covers what Eisenhower lacks; the missing axis is *necessity to a goal*, not urgency.
- **Rumelt's guiding policy** and **Shape Up's default "no"** for unshaped ideas are the strategy-level versions of the same filter.

## 6. Existing tools

**Synthesis.** Every tool models goals as a container with progress rollup; none models necessity, assumptions, or tripwires. What falls flat in practice is consistent: the link is one-directional (goal → work, not work → goal), rollup counts tasks rather than evidence, and goals become a parallel system nobody reviews.

- **Asana Goals**: supporting work is projects/portfolios; task-level linking exists only from the goal side, not from the task, and users have been requesting the reverse since 2023 through April 2026 ([forum 119975](https://forum.asana.com/t/are-the-goals-linked-to-the-tasks-in-asana-or-are-these-like-2-parallel-systems/119975), [forum 1052785](https://forum.asana.com/t/improved-linking-of-tasks-to-goals/1052785)). A task can't tell you what goal it serves. Workaround: a multi-homed project per goal.
- **Todoist Goals**: experimental beta, Pro+, progress bar; an AI goal planner is "planned" ([Todoist help](https://www.todoist.com/help/articles/introduction-to-goals-6DWxzyW2Z)).
- **Linear**: strict initiative → project → issue nesting; progress auto-rolled from project completion ([Linear docs](https://linear.app/docs/sub-initiatives)). Rollup is count-based.
- **Notion**: OKR databases via relations + rollups; consistency is on you ([notion.vip OKRs](https://www.notion.vip/insights/achieve-your-goals-okrs-in-notion)).
- **Sunsama**: weekly objectives attached to tasks plus estimate-vs-actual review — the closest consumer tool to a lead-measure loop ([morgen comparison](https://www.morgen.so/blog-posts/sunsama-vs-motion)). **Motion**: auto-scheduling by deadline/priority, no real goal layer. **Amazing Marvin**: goals exist, link mechanics undocumented ([toolradar](https://toolradar.com/compare/goals-vs-amazing-marvin)).
- **Obsidian/PARA**: PARA organizes by actionability, not goal ([Obsidian forum rollup thread](https://forum.obsidian.md/t/dataview-rollup-for-project-management/34430)).
- **LLM planners**: academic work is surveys and hybrid LLM-plus-symbolic-planner designs; one paper proposes a persistent self-updating task tree ([arXiv personalized agents survey](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.22680v1), [ScienceDirect Thought Management System](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1877750325002170)). Commercial "AI task managers" are calendar packers ([Morgen roundup](https://www.morgen.so/blog-posts/ai-task-manager)).

## Cross-cutting observations

1. **Necessity, not priority, is the missing axis.** WSJF answers "what first"; nothing in the current system answers "does any goal require this at all." The only framework with a per-node test is Dettmer's Goal Tree ("can I reach the goal without this? then cut it"), and impact mapping's actor/impact chain is a lighter equivalent. A task that reaches no goal via a necessity edge is a candidate for Drucker's question.
2. **The path to an outcome goal is a hypothesis, so model it as one.** CPM-style graphs break because the graph is unknown. Theory of change and DDP both say: write the chain of preconditions *and* the assumptions each depends on, then treat the chain as revisable. The "critical path" for landing a client is whichever assumption in that chain has the least evidence.
3. **"De-risk goal X now" = test the top (importance × no-evidence) assumption.** Bland, DDP, OST, and pre-mortems all converge on this. It is mechanically task-generating: assumption → cheapest test → pass/fail threshold → task.
4. **Pre-register the change signal.** Tripwires, kill criteria, decision journals, and "explicit expectancies" all say: write "if state S by date D, revisit" *when you commit*, because you won't recognize it later. This is a field on a goal, not a review habit.
5. **Lead/lag divergence is the strategy-change detector.** Lead strong + lag flat means the theory is wrong; lead weak means execution. Separating the two would let the system say "you're doing the outreach and nothing is converting — the niche or offer is the problem."
6. **Buffer, not due date, per goal.** CCPM's one insight that survives knowledge work: give a goal an appetite (Shape Up) or buffer and watch consumption. Per-task dates on outcome goals are fiction; the existing no-invented-due-dates rule already points this way.
7. **One crux at a time.** Rumelt and Keller agree that a list of goals is not a strategy; the guiding policy's job is to name what you're *not* doing. An LLM assistant can enforce coherence ("this task serves none of the stated guiding policies") more reliably than it can invent strategy.
8. **Keep the review cadence asymmetric.** Weekly: every goal has a next action, lead measures logged, tripwires checked. Quarterly: grade, re-diagnose, rewrite the guiding policy. Grading weekly produces churn (whatmatters, Spotify).

**Where evidence is thin:** Keller, Koch, Working Backwards, and most tool comparisons are anecdote or marketing. The pre-mortem's "30%" is about idea generation, not accuracy. Strategy maps' causal arrows are unvalidated. Nothing here has been evaluated on a single person; the solo-fit judgments are the researcher's.
